Text becomes
numbers

A computer cannot read. In ninety minutes, you will watch a sentence become a row of numbers, a program decide whether it is positive or negative, and understand why it gets it right and why it gets it wrong.

Every block opens with a question. Think (or vote in the chat) before you open the Reveal panel. The fun is in getting it wrong first.
Block 1

Similar to whom?

In the previous class, a 150 g fruit and a 151 g fruit were neighbours on the chart: numbers that were close meant similar things. Your phone's autocorrect thinks the same way about letters: it turns "bad" into "bag" because only one letter changes. Does text work like that?

In each pair, are the two phrases similar or different?
Vote on all four pairs.
Reveal

By letters, "bad" and "not bad" are almost twins, and "great" and "excellent" have little in common. It makes sense to measure that way: it is what autocorrect does.

But meaning goes the other way. Four letters flip the phrase; seven different letters change nothing; swapping the two halves of a sentence moves twenty-six letters and keeps the meaning. With the fruit, close in numbers was close in meaning. In text, similar in form is not similar in meaning.

That is why text is a problem of its own inside machine learning, with its own name: natural language processing, or NLP. And it is the question of this class: if not even the distance between letters works, how do we turn a sentence into numbers a computer can use?

Block 2

The word list

We have 1,984 sentences taken from online reviews of products and restaurants, each already labelled positive or negative. You read a restaurant review and know in two seconds whether the person liked it. Can you write that down as a rule?

Write the words that give away a positive review and the ones that give away a negative one. How many of the 1,984 will your list get right?
Edit the lists, make your guess and press the button.
Reveal

It looked easy: there are only so many words people use to praise or to complain.

The starting list gets 34% of the reviews right, gets under 4% wrong, and has no answer for 62%. The problem is not getting things wrong: it is staying silent, because people write in a thousand ways ("the food came out in five minutes", "waited an hour for a table"). In 2002, researchers from Cornell and IBM ran the same test on movie reviews: word lists written by people got 58% and 64% right.

It is block 1 of the previous class all over again: hand-written rules do not scale. There, the way out was to give examples with answers and let the computer fill in the formula. But a classifier wants a table of numbers: one row per example, one column per measurement, as with the fruit in the previous class. What is the column of a sentence?

Block 3

The table

The simplest idea possible: every different word that shows up in the 1,984 reviews becomes a column. A review's row has a 1 in the columns of the words it uses and a 0 in all the others. For now, a "word" is whatever sits between two spaces.

Guess: how many columns does this table have? And in the row of the sentence you write, how many cells are zero?
Loading the reviews…
Reveal

The intuition is that the table would have a few hundred columns: nobody uses that many words.

It has 4,977 columns. A review uses, on average, 10.1 different words: 99.80% of the row is zero. And 66% of the columns show up in a single review. The order is gone too: "fast delivery but it arrived broken" and "it arrived broken but fast delivery" are the same row, like the lyrics of a song with the lines shuffled, still using the same words.

The list of all the different words is called the vocabulary; the sentence's row is a vector; and this way of building the table, throwing the words in a bag and writing down only which ones are there, is the bag of words. A table made almost entirely of zeros is sparse. Three costs are on the table: too many columns, most of them with a single example, and lost order. The next three blocks go after the first two; the third comes back in block 8.

Block 4

Pieces

When you search for "Great Restaurant" or "great restaurant", Google gives you the same results. Our table is not that smart: look at its columns.

How many different columns did the word "great" get spread across?
Loading the reviews…
Reveal

The usual guesses are one or two.

It is 11 columns: great (69 times), Great (46), "great." (22), "great," (11), "great!" (6), GREAT (1)… Each with few examples, and each learned separately. The same word, spread over 11 columns, splits its examples into 11 pieces. With everything lowercase and letters only, the table drops from 4,977 to 3,187 columns (−36%); removing accents barely matters in English: 3,185.

Cutting text into pieces is tokenization, and each piece is a token. Making different forms the same (lowercase, no punctuation, no accents) is normalization. It is not a cleaning ritual: it gathers into one column the examples that were scattered.

Block 5

Empty words

Back in the days of the telegram, every word cost money, and people cut the little ones: "arriving tuesday train five". Of the 21,000 words in these reviews, 49% are words like that, the kind that show up everywhere.

Tap the words you would throw away because they say nothing about liking or disliking.
Loading the reviews…
Reveal

Throwing away what shows up everywhere looks like pure savings.

The ready-made list cuts 49% of the text, but only 150 columns out of 3,187: a few words, repeated many times. And the list includes "not", the eleventh most common word in these reviews and one of the most telling: it shows up in 189 of the 992 negative reviews and in 32 of the 992 positive ones. "i did not like it" becomes "like"; "i do not recommend it" becomes "recommend".

These lists of frequent words are called stopwords. They were made for search, where "the" and "of" really do get in the way. For sentiment, a common word is not an empty word. How much this costs in accuracy, we measure in block 8.

Block 6

Cutting the ending

love, loved, loves, loving: four columns for the same idea. One way out is to cut off the end of each word and keep only the beginning, like looking up a word's family in the dictionary.

For each pair, will the two words land in the same column after the cut?
Loading the cutting rules…
Reveal

It looks as if cutting the ending gathers the words of the same family, and only those.

The cut is a list of rules about endings, not a dictionary: it joins liked with likely, general with generous and news with new, and keeps good apart from better and went apart from go. Even so, it removes 20% of the columns: from 3,187 to 2,554.

This is stemming, and the piece that is left is the stem. The stemmer on this page is Porter's, published by Martin Porter in 1980, in its original version.

"better" is the comparative of "good" in "the phones were better" and of "well" in "she sings better". The dictionary form depends on the sentence, and the tool picked "well" exactly where it was "good".

Finding the dictionary form is lemmatization, and the form it finds is the lemma. It joins what the cut cannot (went with go, was with were), but it needs a dictionary and context, costs more, and still makes mistakes.

Block 7

How hard each word pulls

In the previous class, the model was a formula with blanks, price = a × area + b, and learning meant filling in the blanks. Here the formula has one blank per column: how hard each word pulls the review toward positive or toward negative. A sentence's score is the sum of how hard its words pull. What is left is figuring out how to fill in 3,187 blanks. On your phone's app store, you already do this by eye: you skim the comments and feel which way they lean.

The math, in Python

For each class, the classifier stores in how many reviews each word shows up (plus 1, so no word is left at zero), divided by the total. The weight is the difference between the logarithms of the two classes: positive if the word is more common in positive reviews.

import re
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

words = lambda t: re.findall(r"[^\W\d_]+(?:'[^\W\d_]+)?", t.lower())
vectorizer = CountVectorizer(analyzer=words, binary=True)   # 1 if the word is there
X = vectorizer.fit_transform(texts)
model = MultinomialNB().fit(X, labels)        # counts the reviews with each word

weights = model.feature_log_prob_[1] - model.feature_log_prob_[0]
print(round(weights[vectorizer.vocabulary_["money"]], 2))   # -2.47
Reveal

We judge a word by what it means: "money" is neutral, and so is "town".

The computer does not know what any word means. It only counted where each one showed up: "money" appeared in 24 negative reviews, almost always as "waste of money"; "town" came from "best tacos in town"; "the" is everywhere and weighs almost nothing. The weight comes from where the word appeared, not from what it means. A sentence's score is the sum of the weights, and with that it gets 81.3% of the reviews it did not see in training right.

Each of these numbers is a weight: it is the "a" of the previous class's formula, one per column. Learning, here, is counting. This classifier is called Naive Bayes. Naive because it adds up the words as if each one had nothing to do with its neighbours, which already tells you where it will go wrong.

Block 8

Your sentence

All the pieces are on the table: cut into pieces, make everything lowercase, maybe remove the empty words, maybe cut the endings, turn it into a row, add up the weights. Anyone who has talked to an automated customer service line knows: sometimes it understands everything, sometimes nothing.

Write a review the classifier gets wrong. Before you look, say why you think it will fail.
Loading the reviews…
Reveal

We expected it to miss irony, and it does. But maybe we did not expect it to miss "not bad" or "no problems", which anyone understands.

Every mistake is written in the numbers. "not" weighs −1.7 and "bad" weighs −3.4: added together they make a very negative phrase, because the sum does not know that one cancels the other. "the delivery was late" comes out positive by a hair, because "late" showed up in one positive and one negative review and weighs nothing. Cutting the endings flips "oh great, it broke after two days" to positive. Removing the empty words erases the "not" and turns "i do not recommend it" positive. And the average barely moves (81.3% with no options, 79.3% without the empty words, worse in 18 of 20 draws) while whole sentences switch sides.

Word pairs ("not_recommend") bring back a little of the order: they are n-grams (bigrams, for pairs). They get more right, 82.4%, better in 15 of 20 draws, and make the table almost five times bigger. No preparation technique is good or bad in general: each one throws something away, and what matters is whether what it threw away was what decided.

Three questions were left open, and they are the road for the next classes:

1. "great" and "excellent" are columns with nothing in common. What if words with similar meanings sat close to each other, like the fruit on the chart? → embeddings
2. "not bad" fails because the sum ignores order. What if the model read each word together with the others in the sentence? → Transformers
3. The weights come from 1,984 reviews of shops and restaurants, in English. "town" is positive here. In movie reviews, in another language or in a mix of two, what changes? → domain and multilingual models
